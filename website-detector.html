Fake sites and safe sites

No Cap does not run a live blocklist — those go stale within hours. What does not go stale is the template. Learn the six shapes below and you will recognise the next one on sight.

Templates that keep working

Addresses shown are illustrative patterns, not real sites.

secure-<bank>-verify.top Brand name sandwiched between security words on a cheap top-level domain. Banks use their own registered domain, never a rented lookalike.
amaz0n-offers.shop Character swap: zero for O, rn for m, l for I. Reads correctly at a glance, resolves somewhere else entirely.
flipkart.com.deals-india.live The real brand placed in a subdomain. Only the last two labels before the slash decide who owns the site — here that is deals-india.live.
http://192.168-style-ip/login A raw IP address instead of a name. No real consumer brand asks you to log in at a number.
govt-refund-claim-portal-online.xyz Keyword stuffing with hyphens. Written for a scam-ad click, not for humans to remember.
bit.ly/xxxxx → ? A shortener hides the destination completely. Expand it before you trust it — several previewers do this for free.

Where to buy and where to check

Established, verifiable destinations. Always type or bookmark them — never arrive from a message.

Genuine products amazon.com · flipkart.com · apple.com · samsung.com · nike.com — or the brand's own site reached through a search engine, not a link.
Accurate information reuters.com · apnews.com · bbc.com · who.int · nasa.gov · rbi.org.in
Fact-checking snopes.com · factcheck.org · fullfact.org · altnews.in
Reputation lookups virustotal.com · urlvoid.com · scamadviser.com · phishtank.org · Google's Safe Browsing status page.
Reporting a scam Your national cybercrime portal, your bank's fraud line, and the brand being impersonated — all three, in that order.
